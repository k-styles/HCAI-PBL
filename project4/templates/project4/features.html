The rule for genres {% info "identifiability" %}

A dimension is only worth having if the responses can ever speak to it.

Two films are drawn at random. For a genre appearing in a fraction p of the pool, they differ on it with probability 2p(1−p) — and a pair that agrees on a genre says nothing at all about its weight. Requiring at least one informative pair in ten puts the cut at p ≥ {{ threshold|floatformat:4 }}.

Western sits at about 0.02, so roughly one pair in twenty-six is informative about it. Across twenty-five responses that is one observation, which is not enough to estimate anything and is enough for the {% term "prior" "prior" %} to invent something.

Unidentifiable at any realistic budget

director_name is 2,398 distinct values and actor_1_name, actor_2_name and actor_3_name add thousands more. Two randomly drawn films share a director well under one time in a thousand, so essentially every response multiplies those weights by zero. They are not weak features; there is no budget at which a participant's answers could ever speak to them.

Not on the card

budget, gross, the four Facebook-likes columns, aspect_ratio, facenumber_in_poster. Nobody picks a film by its aspect ratio, and if a feature is in x but not in front of the participant, the fit will hand it credit for choices it had nothing to do with. Everything in x appears on the card, and everything on the card is in x.

The pool {% info "movies" %}

Filtered before anything else.

Films with fewer than {{ min_votes }} IMDB votes are dropped, and duplicate titles collapsed, leaving {{ films }}. Somebody comparing two films they have never heard of is not expressing a preference — they are reading the metadata off the card and guessing, which adds response noise that no model recovers from. It is a {% term "validity" "validity" %} decision, not a data-cleaning one.
